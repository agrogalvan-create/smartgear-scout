How to size a home booster pump without guessing from horsepower

Independent editorial guide · November 2026. This is a planning checklist, not a site-specific engineering design or a product test.

There are no affiliate links, paid placements, or product rankings in this article.

1. Establish the actual water demand

Estimate the flow rate needed when fixtures are used simultaneously, not the sum of every fixture in the building. Record the desired flow in gallons per minute (GPM) or liters per minute (L/min). A pump selected only by motor horsepower may provide the wrong flow at the required pressure.

2. Measure available inlet pressure and required outlet pressure

Measure supply pressure during realistic use, not only at rest. The booster must supply the pressure difference needed at the intended flow. Check the manufacturer’s minimum inlet-pressure and maximum outlet-pressure requirements.

3. Account for elevation and pipe friction

Vertical elevation consumes approximately 0.433 psi per foot of rise (or 9.81 kPa per meter). Pipes, fittings, filters, check valves and treatment equipment add friction losses that increase with flow. Calculate these losses using the actual installation rather than a universal allowance.

4. Read the pump curve

Identify the operating point where required flow intersects the required pressure increase or head. Confirm that the manufacturer’s curve covers this point without exceeding motor or system limits. Check whether the pump is constant-speed or variable-speed and how it behaves at low demand.

5. Check the water source first

A booster cannot create sustainable water supply where inlet flow is inadequate. For a well, verify well yield, drawdown and protection against dry running. For a municipal connection, verify local water utility rules: direct suction from the public main may be prohibited or require a break tank, backflow prevention or other protection.

6. Protect the plumbing and the pump

  • Verify the pressure rating of pipes, fixtures, tanks and valves.
  • Review thermal protection, dry-run protection, pressure relief and expansion control.
  • Plan service access, noise management, electrical protection and safe drainage.
  • Check local plumbing and electrical codes and use qualified installers where required.

Simple planning example

Suppose the required outlet pressure at a chosen flow is 50 psi and measured inlet pressure under that flow is 25 psi. The basic differential is 25 psi before adding any elevation or friction losses. This does not identify a pump model: the flow rate and full system curve are still needed.

Before buying

Ask for the manufacturer’s published pump curve, inlet operating range, electrical requirements, maximum system pressure, warranty and authorized service options. Do not assume a advertised “maximum pressure” is available at your required flow.
